Get a Grip Household: help

Short on purpose. Read the first part, dip into the rest when you need it. Last updated 25 November 2026.

On this page The idea in three paragraphs How things work For Josh: how it is built

The idea in three paragraphs

The Get a grip approach

Every day, make progress on one big task and fit in as many small ones as you can. Big tasks are the things that actually move your life forward: the tax return, redoing the bathroom. Small tasks keep it running. Rather than show you the whole mountain, each morning Get a grip builds a short menu from your own lists: one bite of a big task, with a few small things around it. A big task is broken into steps, so today you do one step, not the whole job. When you find yourself with a spare quarter of an hour, the Slot button asks how long you have and what you have with you, and finds something that fits. There are no scores and no streaks, and the only statement of importance is where something sits in your own list. What you didn’t get to simply stays where it was.

The household bit

Running a home produces an endless pile of jobs between two people, and the trouble is usually in the planning and the dividing up, not the doing. So the household side does two things. First, a structured planning session you do together on a bigger screen: get everything out of your heads area by area, group the notes into jobs, sort them into Big and Small, and put each list in order of importance. Second, share the jobs out. Every job goes to one person, never both, who owns it from start to finish.

How they fit together

Planning is done together, at the start and now and then afterwards. The doing is done alone, every day, on your own phone. The jobs shared out to you arrive there as ordinary Get a grip tasks, mixed in with your own personal ones, so Today builds your day from both. Your personal tasks stay on your own device and are never part of the shared list, so neither of you sees the other’s.

How things work

Each of these opens and closes.

Planning together: what each round is for

Do it together at one screen, ideally a laptop. One of you types, both of you call things out, and you don’t decide who does what yet. Every idea becomes a sticky note on the wall.

  • Warm up: six questions to shake things loose.
  • Walk each area: a tile for each part of life (House, Cleaning, Garden, Major bills, Money, Car, Kids, Health, Business ventures, Trips & fun, Paperwork), plus “Add your own”. House, Cleaning, Garden, Car and Paperwork go part by part, room by room for example. The others go month by month through the coming year, because that is how bills and dates arrive. Tap an example to add it, or type your own. The two-minute round is a timer for a quick burst.
  • The year ahead: a general sweep, month by month.
  • Regular payments: see its own section below.
  • Group, sort and add details: where the pile of notes becomes jobs (next section).

Skip any round you like. “Show me an example wall” fills the wall with samples so you can see how it works, and “Remove the example notes” clears them again.

Group, sort and add details: the board

The last round shows two lists, Big and Small. The order in each list is the priority, with the most important at the top. Prioritising is simply putting things in the order you want them.

  • Big or small is your call, and the app never changes it for you. A small job that gains steps stays small until you drag it across. Roughly: big needs steps or takes days, small is done in one go.
  • Drag up or down within a list to reorder. On a phone use the ▲ ▼ arrows. The order is shared, and it carries into each person’s own Big and Small lists.
  • Drag to the other list to change a job from Big to Small or back.
  • Drop a job on the middle of another to make it a step of that job. It becomes the last step and takes that job’s area and owner. Drop it on the top edge to put it above instead. If the job you drop already has steps of its own, they all move across in order with their ticks, its details are added to the other job’s, and the emptied job disappears.
  • Not now moves a job out of the way without deleting it. “Bring back” returns it.

Tap a job’s words to open its set-up panel (next section).

The set-up panel: everything you can say about a job

Tap any job or step on the board or on Share out. The phone’s edit screen has the same fields.

  • What it is, size, area and whose job. The area decides which activity the job lives in on the phone (🏠 House, 🚗 Car and so on).
  • Part of a big job. Put a small job under a big one, move it to another, or choose None to stand it on its own again. “Move its steps into a big job” does the same for a small job that has steps. On the phone you can only choose big jobs from the same side (household with household, personal with personal), so nothing becomes shared by accident.
  • Steps. One level only: a step can’t have steps. A small job finishes itself when its last step is ticked. A big job is only ever finished by you.
  • How long it takes and Needs at least (nothing, phone, tablet or laptop). These feed the Slot finder.
  • Details. Free text. Web addresses and phone numbers become tappable in the list on the phone.
  • Wait. A day, a week, a month, a date, or until another job is finished. The job stays in its lists but is left out of your day until then.
  • Due date, Comes into the list and Repeats: see “Due dates, reminders and repeats”.
Sharing out

Every job goes to one person, never both, because two people who each own a job each assume the other will do it. Drag a card to Lauren’s or Josh’s column, or tap the round L or J on the card. The strip at the top shows how many big and small jobs each of you holds. It is there for a quick look together, not as a score. You can reassign a job whenever you like, and its steps travel with it.

Your day on the phone (My day)

My day is Get a grip itself. Today builds a short menu: how many big tasks (none to three), how many steps of each, how many small ones. Automatic takes the first big job in your Big list and the first small ones from your Small list. Custom lets you choose. Slot asks how long you have and what you have with you, then offers things that fit. Once a day is built, Refresh rebuilds it from your lists as they are now, Finish closes the day with an optional note, and Redo clears it so you can start again.

The lists behind it: Big and Small are in your own order (drag to change; the first big job is what Automatic picks), Master is a read-only list of what you could do next, and Activities are your areas of life. Household jobs sit in an activity named after their area, next to your personal ones.

The buttons at the top, Everything, Household and Personal, filter the lists and also what Automatic and Slot pick from. Choose Household to work only on the shared jobs, Personal for only your own, or Everything for the lot. The choice is remembered on that device. The ⋮ menu holds the housekeeping: Tag tasks, Archive, backup and restore, appearance, the Today layout, and “Plan together” to get back to the planning pages.

Due dates, reminders and repeats (the quiet way)

Some household jobs really do have dates: car tax, the TV licence, the MOT. But a wall of overdue markers makes people feel they have failed before they begin, so due dates are kept quiet. They show only inside a job, never in the lists, and nothing is ever marked overdue. Instead:

  • Comes into the list. A dated job can stay out of sight until a chosen time before it is due: a week, two weeks, four weeks or eight weeks. Four weeks is the default for regular payments. When its time comes it arrives at the top of the list, not somewhere down the bottom. Big jobs work the same way. It is still visible on the Activities page.
  • Reminders. A phone notification comes a week before and the day before, only to the person the job belongs to.
  • Due within a week and the job goes to the top of what Automatic picks for Today.
  • Repeats. Every week, month, 3 months, 6 months, year or 2 years. When you tick a repeating job the next one appears with its date moved on.

To turn reminders on (Android): on the Devices page, with the phone linked to the household, tap “Turn on reminders”, then Allow when Chrome asks, then “Send a test reminder”. A notification should appear within a few seconds. Do this on each person’s own phone.

Regular payments from your email

Round 4 of Brainstorm finds the things that come round again and need paying, renewing or booking, so nobody has to remember them.

  1. Tap Copy the prompt and paste it into an AI that can read your Gmail (Claude with Gmail connected, for example).
  2. The AI searches two years of email and shows you what it found. Agree the list with it in the chat: drop anything that doesn’t matter. Automatic payments that need no decision are left out.
  3. When you’re happy, it gives you a small file, recurring.md. For each item it includes how to renew or pay with the same supplier (a web address, a phone number, where the renewals come from) and the reference they ask for, so nobody has to search again.
  4. Bring the file in with “Choose recurring.md…”, or paste its contents.
  5. Set up each item: whose job it is, small or big (changing insurer is a big one), and how long before it is due it should come into the list. “Add all as they are” adds everything with the settings shown. The jobs land in Share out, or straight in the chosen person’s column.

Bringing in a newer file never adds the same item twice. Policy and customer references are fine to include. Passwords, PINs, card numbers and bank details never are.

Linking devices: what the household code is

The household code is a key to one drawer in the shared store: a block of 12 random letters and numbers, such as K7QD-4XMB-92TH. There are no accounts and no passwords.

  1. Creating it. On the Devices page tap “Create a household code”. Your browser makes the code and puts your list in a new drawer labelled with it.
  2. Linking another device. On the other device, open Devices, type the code and tap “Link this device”. It fetches the drawer, merges it with anything it already has, and is linked.
  3. Staying in step. Every device keeps a full copy. It checks the drawer when you open the app, when you come back to it, a second after any change, and every 20 seconds while it is open. Changes are merged job by job (the newest edit wins, and deletions are remembered), so two people can work at once.

The code is not a login. Anyone who has it can read and change the list, so keep it between the two of you. Any linked device shows the code on its Devices page, so you only need to find it again if all of them are gone. “Unlink this device” stops that device syncing but leaves its copy. The only way to cancel a code is to start a new household.

Phone or computer, Household view and the PIN

Each device says whose it is (Lauren or Josh) and, from its first opening, whether it is a phone or a computer. A phone is for doing: it opens on My day and keeps the planning pages behind “Plan together” in its ⋮ menu. A computer is for planning together: it opens on Brainstorm. You can change it on the Devices page.

Household view hides My day and every personal task, for when you look at the household together on one person’s own computer. The first time you use it you set a PIN, and you need that PIN to leave Household view. The PIN is stored on that device only. Five wrong tries means a 30-second wait. If you forget it, “Reset this device” removes the PIN and the personal tasks kept on that device, so a forgotten PIN never exposes anything. Household jobs come back from the shared list.

A PIN is a curtain, not a lock. It stops a casual look, but the tasks are still held in the browser on that device, so keep the device itself private.

Where your information is kept

Personal tasks are kept only in the browser on the device you typed them on. They are never part of the shared list and never sent anywhere. The household list is kept on each linked device and in one shared copy in a small store on Cloudflare, under your household code. The store belongs to the app’s owner, who could in principle read it, so don’t put passwords, card numbers or bank details in it. Supplier references for renewals are fine. The privacy page says all this in plain English.

Backups. The ⋮ menu on the planning pages has “Download a backup” of the household list. My day’s menu has backup and restore for the tasks on that device. There is no restore button for the household list yet.

When something looks wrong

A job isn’t on my phone. Check the phone is linked (Devices shows the code), that the job has been shared out to you, and the Everything / Household / Personal buttons. A dated job may not be due to come in yet. The Activities page shows everything. “Sync now” on Devices forces a check.

We see different lists. Both devices must be linked with the same code. Give it 20 seconds, or tap “Sync now”.

The app looks out of date. ⋮ More, then “Check for a new version”, then “Update now” when it appears. The version number is at the foot of the page.

Reminders don’t arrive. They are set up for Chrome on Android, on a phone linked to the household, with notifications allowed for the site. Use “Send a test reminder” on Devices. They are only sent for jobs with a due date that belong to you.

I forgot my PIN, or lost the code. See “Phone or computer, Household view and the PIN” and “Linking devices”.

For Josh: how it is built, how to fork it, how to scramble personal tasks

Written for a developer. Everything here is in the repository at github.com/DMHMca/get-a-grip-household, and CLAUDE.md in it is the full project brief and decision log.

The shape of it

No libraries and no build step: two static HTML files with inline CSS and vanilla JavaScript, one service worker, and one Cloudflare Worker. Hosting is GitHub Pages (branch main, root). The app shares the origin dmhmca.github.io with the owner’s personal Get a grip, so every storage key and cache name is prefixed to keep the two apart.

FileWhat it is
index.htmlThe planning side: Brainstorm, Share out, Devices, the ⋮ More panel, the detail panel, the PIN.
tasks.htmlMy day: a fork of Get a grip (Today, Big, Small, Master, Activities and every edit screen) plus the household link that brings a person’s household jobs in and sends changes back.
sw.jsService worker. Pages are fetched network-first with cache:'no-cache' so a release shows at the next open. It also handles push and notification clicks.
worker/worker.jsThe Cloudflare Worker: the shared store, the reminder sign-ups, and the daily reminder job.
privacy.html, help.htmlThis page and the privacy page.
tests/Scripted-browser checks and a runner (tests/run.sh, see tests/README.md).

Data

  • household.doc.v2: the shared document {tasks, gone, day, cats, triage}. Each task has Get a grip’s own fields (text, size, parent, done, wait/wf/wh, dur, dev, notes, updated) plus to (0 Lauren, 1 Josh, null unshared), area, r (priority rank), due, rep (week, month, 3m, 6m, year, 2y) and lead (days before the due date that it comes into the lists).
  • household.local.v2: this device’s settings: code, me, kind, together, pin/pinSalt/pinWho, push, plus where you were in the planning rounds.
  • gagh.*: My day’s own data and settings, behind the LS wrapper in tasks.html. Personal tasks live here and nowhere else.
  • On the phone each household job is mirrored into an activity hh:<area>. Only tasks in those activities are ever written back to the shared document (isHH, hhPull, hhPush).

Sync

A device syncs on open, on return to the tab, 1.2 seconds after a change, and every 20 seconds while visible. It GETs /h/<code>, merges with its local document, and PUTs {base, doc} only if the merged result differs (stable() compares them). The merge keeps the later updated per task and per day/cats/triage entry and honours gone tombstones for 90 days. KV is eventually consistent and has no atomic compare-and-set, so the version check is best effort. A rare lost update heals itself on the next cycle, because every device merges its own state back in.

The Worker

RouteWhat it does
GET /h/<CODE>Returns {v, doc} or 404.
PUT /h/<CODE>Body {base, doc}. Saves only if base equals the stored version, otherwise 409 with the current copy. Limit 400,000 characters.
POST /sub|unsub|test/<CODE>Reminder sign-up, removal, and a test push. Endpoints are checked against a short list of real push hosts.
POST /msgThe service worker collects the waiting reminder text for its own subscription (then it is deleted).
cron 7 8 * * *Once a day (UTC) every household with a signed-up phone is checked. Jobs due in 7 days or 1 day send a reminder to the owning person’s phones.

KV keys: h:<code> (the document), subs:<code>, msg:<hash> (queued text, expires in 7 days), sent:… (de-duplication). Reminders use VAPID: the push is sent empty, signed with the secret VAPID_PRIVATE (a JWK as a JSON string), and the phone’s service worker fetches the text from /msg. That avoids payload encryption. Access control is only that the code is hard to guess (12 characters from a 32-symbol alphabet, about 60 bits) and the Worker cannot list drawers.

Releasing and testing

Bump APP_VERSION in both HTML files (H1.9, H1.10 … H1.99, then H2.0) and the cache name in sw.js. Run tests/run.sh, which needs Node, Python and Playwright, and push to main. The checks print what they saw, so read the output as well as the pass or fail. A real phone, a real notification and a real AI reading a real mailbox have not been tested by them.

How to fork it

  1. Fork the repository on GitHub and switch on GitHub Pages for main. Your site will be at https://<you>.github.io/<repo>/.
  2. In Cloudflare create a KV namespace and a Worker. Paste in worker/worker.js. Bind the namespace to the Worker with the variable name HOUSEHOLD. Add a cron trigger 7 8 * * *.
  3. Generate a VAPID key pair (below). Store the private half as a Worker secret named VAPID_PRIVATE, and never commit it. Put the public half in worker/worker.js (VAPID_PUBLIC) and in index.html (VAPID_PUBLIC).
  4. In worker/worker.js set ORIGINS to your Pages origin (CORS). In index.html set SERVER, and in tasks.html set HH.SERVER, to your Worker’s address.
  5. Change VAPID_SUBJECT in the Worker to your own site, and update the privacy page and the About links.
  6. Open the site, create a household code on Devices, link a second device with it, and check a note travels. Then turn on reminders and use “Send a test reminder”.

I deployed the Worker through the Cloudflare API (a multipart upload with main_module and the HOUSEHOLD and VAPID_PRIVATE bindings, then a schedules call). The dashboard route above is the usual alternative, but I haven’t tried it.

Scrambling personal tasks

Today there is nothing to scramble: personal tasks never leave the device. Personal sync between a person’s own devices is not built. The design below is what I would do, and none of it is tested.

  • Separate drawer, separate code. Each person gets a personal code that is never the household code. Add a second Worker route such as /p/<id> that copies the /h GET/PUT/409 logic with a larger size limit. The drawer id is a hash of the code, so the store never sees the code itself.
  • Encrypt on the device. Derive an AES-GCM key from the code, encrypt the personal data with a fresh random IV, and upload only {iv, ct}. The store, and anyone with the Cloudflare login, then holds only ciphertext.
  • Leave the household jobs out. Send everything except activities starting hh, and none of the household.* keys. The household code already covers those, and mixing them would duplicate jobs.
  • Reuse the merge. tasks.html still contains Get a grip’s Drive sync engine (mergeDocs, driveCycle, applyMerged, with gone and ord for deletions and ordering). Hide the Google bits and swap its transport for these calls.
  • Make the code longer. The 12-character code is fine as a lookup key, but anyone holding the ciphertext can guess offline. For an encryption key use 20 or more random characters (around 100 bits), or add a passphrase.
  • Lose the code, lose the cloud copy. Devices keep their own copies, but nothing can recover the drawer.
// sketch only: personal sync encryption (WebCrypto)
const enc = new TextEncoder(), dec = new TextDecoder();
const b64 = u8 => btoa(String.fromCharCode(...u8));
const unb64 = s => Uint8Array.from(atob(s), ch => ch.charCodeAt(0));
async function keyFrom(code) {
  const base = await crypto.subtle.importKey('raw', enc.encode(code), 'PBKDF2', false, ['deriveKey']);
  return crypto.subtle.deriveKey(
    { name: 'PBKDF2', salt: enc.encode('gagh-personal-v1'), iterations: 310000, hash: 'SHA-256' },
    base, { name: 'AES-GCM', length: 256 }, false, ['encrypt', 'decrypt']);
}
async function drawerId(code) {                 // what the store sees instead of the code
  const h = await crypto.subtle.digest('SHA-256', enc.encode('drawer:' + code));
  return [...new Uint8Array(h)].slice(0, 16).map(b => b.toString(16).padStart(2, '0')).join('');
}
async function seal(code, obj) {
  const iv = crypto.getRandomValues(new Uint8Array(12));
  const ct = await crypto.subtle.encrypt({ name: 'AES-GCM', iv }, await keyFrom(code), enc.encode(JSON.stringify(obj)));
  return { iv: b64(iv), ct: b64(new Uint8Array(ct)) };
}
async function unseal(code, blob) {
  const pt = await crypto.subtle.decrypt({ name: 'AES-GCM', iv: unb64(blob.iv) }, await keyFrom(code), unb64(blob.ct));
  return JSON.parse(dec.decode(pt));
}

The household list cannot be scrambled the same way without losing reminders. The Worker has to read the due dates and owners to send them. Scrambling it would mean dropping server-side reminders, or sending the Worker a separate small reminders index.
